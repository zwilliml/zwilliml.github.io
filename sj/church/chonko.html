The following article is from the July 25, 2008, edition of The Catholic Exponent:

Fr. Michael Chonko: 'I always say, "Lord, be right here with me. Help me through this day'"

For Father Michael J. Chonko, retirement means continuing his work as an instrument of God. At age 95, Father Chonko is the oldest priest in the Youngstown Diocese. Retired since June 1, 1977, Father Chonko said, "I'm exceptionally blessed with good health." He currently lives in a cottage east of New Castle, Pa., which he bought in 1954.

Purchase of that property was part refuge and part planning for the future. "I was raised in the country and I wanted a retreat from the city. I also wanted to retire where I could enjoy God's nature," said Father Chonko. Noting that many people don't plan for the future, Father Chonko attributes a satisfying retirement to sound advice and well-defined goals.

Independence is important to this gourmet cook, who still hunts deer and goes fishing. "I catch my own trout in the stream right behind my house," he said. Remaining active is also vital. "I can't see sitting around when there are things to be done," Father Chonko said.

A physics/chemistry/math teacher for 27 years and a parish priest, Father Chonko originally planned a career in medicine. "I was educated by the Benedictines and I saw these men who were holy, pious and intellectual, so I left pre-med." He was ordained a Benedictine monk in 1943. He volunteered to serve at St. John the Baptist Parish in Campbell in 1950 when a priest fluent in the Slovak language was needed. "The good Lord blessed me with many responsibilities and talents to do things for the people for His greater honor and glory and for the benefit and salvation of all souls," said Father Chonko.

Father Chonko was incardinated as a priest of the Youngstown Diocese in 1958. His last assignment before retirement was as pastor of St. Joseph Parish in Newton Falls. Saying he cannot believe it's been 31 years since he left St. Joseph, he noted, "God still uses me in many ways." On many weekends, Father Chonko drives to celebrate Mass at parishes in New Castle and Youngstown, assists troubled families, preaches at Forty Hours and fills in for other priests when needed. "A lot of people call me to visit their parents because I know the Slovak language. As long as God blesses me with good health, I'll help."

Father Chonko attributes his longevity to exercise and healthy foods. He was a member of his college diving and swimming teams, coached the golf and rifle teams during his 14 years at Ursuline High School, and is a certified lifeguard. Having taught chemistry, Father Chonko knows the consequences of poor eating habits. "I always told my students, 'God gave you one little machine [your body] to take care of; don't put garbage in it and you'll have a long life.'"

The priest's days begin with prayers, meditation, Mass and thanksgiving. "Whenever I do anything I always say 'Lord be right here with me. Help me through this day.'" Father Chonko spends time with God through everyday tasks such as walking to the mailbox. "Retirement has given me much more time to meditate, to spiritualize my life," said Father Chonko. "Every day I'm doing little things for penance for my past sins."

As a child Father Chonko was trained to feed his spiritual side through good deeds, penance, kindness, love and forgiveness. "That's still the way I live as a retired priest," he said.

The following article by Lou Jacquet is from the March 28, 2003, edition of The Catholic Exponent:

EVERYDAY FAITH
At 90, there's no rocking chair in this priest's plans

In about 37 years, God willing, I will be 90 years old. I hope to be alive and well, with reasonably intact mental faculties. My current idea of what I might be able to accomplish at that age involves extended periods of sitting relatively upright in a rocking chair, frequent and unencumbered access to adjacent bathroom facilities, and long morning and afternoon naps between meals at the nursing home.

That scenario is clearly not what being 90 means to Father Michael Chonko. I was privileged to attend the Mass for his 60th anniversary as a priest March 16 at Campbell St. John Parish. To watch Father Chonko celebrate that liturgy - complete with vigorous swinging of the thurible around the altar and moving with agility up and down numerous steps - is to wonder anew at the genes some among us are blessed with. Could this man have possibly been ordained months before the Diocese of Youngstown was even established in 1943?

This was a day for remembering, beginning with the homily by Father George Franko and continuing through remarks by the jubilarian himself. Father Chonko recalled losing his father in a coal-mining accident as a youngster of 10; growing up in a house with no electricity, heat, running water or indoor plumbing; being called to priesthood as the result, he firmly believes, of frequent prayers for a religious vocation in his family. But he is not mired in the past. He keeps up with, and not infrequently shares his views on, a variety of current issues.

It is no news that these are difficult days for the Catholic priesthood. We hear, no doubt because of the horrific nature of their crimes of abuse against children, about the tainted dozens who have failed to live up to their vows. These crimes must be reported and cannot go unpunished when those charged are found to be guilty.

In such a milieu, however, we hear too little of the countless years of service to the Church by thousands of faithful priests. That service has often extended well beyond the call of duty; in his case, Father Chonko made sure that all the students in his parishes who wanted to attend Catholic grade school and high school did so at parish expense. Who among us cannot tell a story of some priest who shared a moment of great significance with our family at a baptism, First Communion, wedding, by a sickbed or at the death of a loved one?

By living to 90, Father Chonko has been blessed to reap the reward of thanks and gratitude which many other priests who died too young rarely got to hear. The same must be said for women religious, brothers and deacons who have gone on before us all too soon. They too, served selflessly, but as the late comedian Red Buttons would have it, they "never got a dinner."

Is every priest, nun, brother or deacon a model of deep holiness? Hardly. The human condition afflicts us all. But for every one among these who has hurt someone by indifference, arrogance, or sinfulness, there must be a thousand more who have shaped lives, helped to shoulder burdens, stood against injustice or led the way along the faith journey.

Not all who have worked in these ministries have survived to mark 60 years of service. Not all possess Father Chonko's energy level and good health. I'm just pleased that, this time, those present were able to pay tribute before the accolades came during a memorial service. We should do more praising and thanking while those who serve our Church are still walking among us, don't you think?

Lou Jacquet is editor of the Exponent
